Week 9 — Greenlining and Native American Property Rights

One

A 50 year old data center for NASDAQ in my hometown has been bought and is going to be converted into an AI data center. Trumbull, CT has placed a 2 year moratorium on data centers, so it will not be converted just yet. Lots of people in my town have vocalized a strong dissent for it, and there have been petitions to stop the conversion. People’s main concern is the rising energy prices that may occur when the grid becomes strained during peak times. Local residents already fear electricity prices are too high as they are now. My first selectmen has made this promise, “I told the applicant directly: no increase in electrical draw beyond existing site capacity, no increase in noise, no increase in water use, no expansion of the building’s footprint. They have agreed to all four.” The other concern is also about the future of the data center if it wants to expand. There is no clear answer how to enforce this, and residents fear the board doesn't have the answers either.

Source: https://ctmirror.org/2026/09/11/trumbull-ai-data-center-moratorium/

Two

  • If a community is disadvantaged it most likely is cheaper to build an environmental hazard such as some energy plant. This makes people not want to live in that area, lowering housing costs. Then, someone with less income is forced to live in the bad area because they can't afford a community that is not environmentally compromised.
  • Another reason may be the difference in cost of a company to build their factory in two different locations. A wealthier community property could cost more than a poorer community’s property. Either the land or even public opposition can be more costly.
  • This is more consistent with the folk belief in environmental economics, which is that firms/companies purposefully target lower income areas to build new factories.

If a community all of a sudden became desirable, the demand curve would shift to the right as many people would want to move into the community. This would increase the price of a house for example, and make it even more difficult for lower income people to move in. Only richer people would be able to afford to move. This later would raise prices for current lower income people living there, and some may be forced to move out. So while you may make the community “safer, cleaner, healthier, and more enjoyable,” the current residents won't be able to afford to live there, and won't reap the benefits.

Two.A

  • Noise pollution is on my mind as an environmental justice issue, but it wouldn't have been one of my first thoughts. Personally, where I live there isn't too much noise pollution, therefore it's not the first thing that comes to mind. If someone lived in a city or main road, however, I think noise pollution would be something they focused more on.
  • Assuming a 5% discount rate, the $110 billion “economic cost of traffic noise” would be $5.5 billion, the socially efficient Pigouvian tax amounts of $974 per vehicle would be $48.7, and the, “noise reduction benefits of $77.3 billion” would be $3.9 billion. $5.5 billion is 0.0138% of the US’s GDP per year, which is not a sizable effect. But, $48.7 per vehicle per year is not much, especially in addition to the $3.9 billion benefit from noise reduction if everyone switched to EV’s.
  • Noise reduction is an example of “greenlining” as it provides the disadvantaged community an environmental benefit. I wouldn't call it simply gentrification. I think gentrification can occur as a result of the greenlining, but making a community quieter is not gentrification as the main purpose is to help residents currently in the community. Environmental justice has been achieved, I believe, as the community is better off. However, the people who receive this benefit may be different than the original residents. Existing homeowners and landlords will see the benefit, but renters who have priced out won't be around for the benefit.
  • We should assist disadvantaged residents in being able to stay as the purpose of environmental justice was for their benefit and making their community better. No matter who lives in the area, we should provide environmental justice, but in the process people shouldn't be forced out of their homes in the name of environmental justice. One idea may be instituting rent control for a few years after the improvement.

In practice, the town can have an agency to measure its air quality and the emissions that come from farms, schools, plants, etc. If one of these places goes over its personal pollution limit, it would be up to the town to enforce their rules and work with the place to bring their levels back down.

Four

The Massachusetts law protecting disadvantaged communities from environmental harm, also makes it more difficult for these communities to grow economically as the amount of progression may be hindered by the law. The intention of the law was to protect these communities from harmful power plants or factories that heavily pollute the area, but in doing so they also hinder the development of these areas as most development falls under the MEPA. As stated in an earlier question, even building a small baseball stadium will trigger MEPA, even though it is very different from a power plant for example. So if a biotech company is looking to invest in a new area, it may pick a non-MEPA location as it will be less costly without having to do the environmental impact report, which can be upwards to $1 million and years of work. So while the purpose behind this law was well-intentioned, it can end up hurting disadvantaged communities from being invested in and grow economically.

Five

  • To evaluate the effects of MEPA to see if they are helpful or harmful we need to compare communities that fall under environmental justice and those that don't.
  • Treated group: 3 environmental justice communities that are similar to each other in size, population, land price, geographic location, demographic characteristics, income.
  • Control group: 3 non-environmental justice communities that are similar to each other in size, population, land price, geographic location, demographic characteristics, income.
  • The treated and controlled communities should all be similar in terms listed above.
  • Outcomes being tracked: air quality, noise pollution, economic progess/regress (number of new projects created, proposed, started)
  • Data needed: Pollution emissions in each town, air quality in each town, # of new projects created, proposed, started
  • Hypothesis: In the next 5 years, communities labeled as environmental justice populations will see less economic growth compared to non-environmental justice populations
  • With this information, we would hopefully see differences between the environmental justice and non-environmental justice populations in terms of their economic development. This would examine if the law has unintentionally harmed these communities in terms of economic growth. In addition, I would track environmental issues such as air quality and noise pollution to see if the law is helping as it intended.

Six

This is extreme, but since the costs and time of an environmental impact report are what seems to be the reason EJ populations are being harmed economically, it may be helpful to level the playing field and have every population in Massachusetts require an environmental impact report. This way, no matter where you build, this is a required cost and shouldn't be the deciding factor for where to build. This focuses on the potential harm economically from the current MEPA law. I don't think this would get passed because this would stunt growth all over Massachusetts. If this happened, companies may not even look at Massachusetts as a viable place to build. Neighboring cities like Providence and Portsmouth may gain more attention from companies looking to invest.

Seven (additional notes)

I don't know much about Native American tribal lands today, the information I learned in school was focused on the history of Native American tribes, not so much as what is happening presently. Based on what I hear from time to time in class or social media, I would say that Native American tribes today do not have as many rights as typical American citizens.

The Dawes Act of 1887 allotted individual Native Americans areas of land, within a trust for usually 25 years. After 25 years, the land was removed out of the government trust and now would be taxed. These costs made it difficult for tribal members to hold onto their land, and they often had to give up their land to settlers. Another issue that occurred was since land was split up into individual allotments and then passed on to heirs, shared ownership made decision-making difficult. If 8 people collectively shared a piece of land, each with ⅛ ownership, deciding to mine or create a recreation opportunity became difficult, getting a majority was hard, time consuming, and costly.

Land acknowledgements simply acknowledge that the land being used was historically a Native American tribal land. At most they mention a, “painful history,” but they often do not accurately describe the wrongdoings. Land acknowledgements fail to acknowledge the personal wrongs of the current institution on the land. By acknowledging they aren't taking responsibility. Even though they might not have been the ones who actually took the land or committed atrocities, they are still reaping the benefits from those that did. So their acknowledgement is basically saying sorry this happened to you, but we’re still going to continue as we are.
