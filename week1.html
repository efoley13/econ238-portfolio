Statistics, Big Numbers & Data Centers

Week 1 explores environmental statistics, the presentation of large numbers, and the economic and environmental questions surrounding data centers.

SECTION 1

As Rare as Our Understanding of Statistics

1A

A common concern may be that it makes it sound like a flood that should happen only once in a thousand years, but flooding that extreme has happened. Other hurricanes like Katrina or Sandy have seen extreme flooding and water surges as well, which were both within the past 25 years. The phrasing exaggerates reality.

1B

A “1,000 year event” means that there is “a 1 in 1,000 chance of occurring in any given year (USGS).” The yearly risk of getting in a car accident is 1 in 70 (1.4%) and the risk of getting the flu in adults 18-64 is 8.8%. Compared to these, a 1000 year event is less likely to occur at only 0.1% chance, yet the general public does not seem to be as concerned about these issues which are also semi-preventable. I think the misconception stems from people reading “1,000 year flood” in their head as “one-in-a-thousand-years”, not as 1/1000 chance in a given year. If the news gave the statistic as a 0.1% chance of flooding, people would recognize this more so as an individualized event. That every year there is a 0.1% chance of flooding this extreme occurring. When thinking of it as “one-in-a-thousand-years”, my initial thought is that this type of event should only occur once out of every thousand years. If the news reports that this is the multiple 1000-year flood we've seen in the past few years, it feels way more concerning in terms of climate change.

1C

i.

Each game a sports team plays is an individualized event so no matter how many games you've won or lost previously has no effect on whether or not you will win/lose. Only two outcomes of a game, 50% chance of winning. Using the same language of the flood example would be as if a team has lost the last ten games and then wins one, saying the odds of them winning that game was one in two years chance. It sounds way more dramatic that way.

ii.

“The recurrence interval (sometimes called the return period) is based on the probability that the given event will be equalled or exceeded in any given year. For example, there is a 1 in 50 chance that 6.60 inches of rain will fall in Mecklenburg County in a 24-hour period during any given year. Thus, a rainfall total of 6.60 inches in a consecutive 24-hour period is said to have a 50-year recurrence interval.”

It is so easy to understand when it is phrased like this because the wording is the same as the math behind the statistic, as you read you also do the math in your head inherently. The confusing way of stating these events seems to try to shorten or simplify beyond easy recognition. The confusing way also evokes more of a reaction than it otherwise would, which may be intentional to get readers to care more.

1D

400 years is a long time, lots has changed geographically. There's been a huge increase in urban development in the past 400 years, we have less farmland and more cities, climate change has also made weather more extreme. It’s not fair to compare flooding from today to 400 years ago when the landscape has changed and flood-prevention systems are in place. The weather itself has changed as well, rain is more intense and abrupt. Also, the classifications of a 1-in-400 year flood changes depending on location. A 1-in-400 flood in Rochester may be less extreme than a 1-in-400 year flood in a city that experiences more extreme flooding due to flatter land or worse engineered solutions. These are blanket statements that are received as equal in magnitude, but aren't transferable across cities or regions.

1E

If each event had a 1% chance of getting in the news, it would be fairly likely to see it. There are so many events every day around the globe, each acting independently of each other. If each continent was grouped separately there would be 7 regions for once in a century events to take place. There are 5 major events that can occur, totalling 35 situations that could occur in that year all at a 1% chance.

SECTION 2

Big Numbers

2A

70 million gallons of water per year sounds like a lot of water is being spent on fracking, but in comparison to yearly household water usage numbers it is fairly regular. The article explains how 514 households in California use roughly 70 million gallons as well. California’s state oil and gas supervisor also explained how not all of the 70 million gallons of water are fresh water that could be used in daily use, and is actually produced in the process of fracking. The original scary big number of 70 million gallons isn't as scary when you apply context and background information that was purposefully left out of the scary headline. Even though in comparison the 70 million gallons of water isn't that bad, the argument of what that 70 million gallons could be used for instead of for natural gas is a genuine concern.

2B

In a New York Times article on data centers in Pittsburgh, PA, there was the claim that “seven of the proposed plants in Pennsylvania could emit roughly 68 million tons of carbon dioxide or equivalent greenhouse gases each year.” The given marginal information was that this is “equivalent to the annual emissions from about 15 million gasoline-burning cars.” The extra marginal information strengthens the argument that the data centers contain environmental repercussions with serious consequences. When placed in more context, I would say the given “boogeyman number” is even more terrifying. Doing the math in terms of car emissions is something familiar enough to many that it holds up in terms of plausibility. There are 8.75 million gasoline-burning cars in all of Pennsylvania, which makes these numbers worse. 7 data centers are going to produce nearly double what all of the gas-burning cars in the state alone produce.

2C

The reporting uses both “big-number boogeyman” and “small-number swindle”, but more so the “big-number boogeyman”. In the second paragraph the author describes the permits that were rejected that would’ve allowed 3,000 gallons of water to be pumped a minute. The article is upfront with how much the foreign-owned farm would potentially be using. This is the main focus of the article, that these foreign-owned farms are using so much of Arizonans water. The “small-number swindle” that 3% of US farmland is controlled by foreign entities is brushed over quickly later in the article, and the focus is returned to the state’s water uses. According to the Arizona Department of Water Resources , agriculture accounts for 72% of the state's water use, municipal/urban accounts for 22%, and industrial for 6%, totaling 7 million acre-feet of water. The foreign-owned farm would use 16,415 acre-feet of water per year (AI), or 0.3% of the total agricultural water use or 1.1% of the urban water use. This additional context weakens the narrative that foreign-owned farms are exploiting our groundwater in the US because they use up such a small percentage of the state's water use. So while the amount of water they are using seems concerning, compared to the total amount of water used by other agricultural farms, it doesn't seem that crazy.

i. Agricultural Water Use

0.72 × 7,000,000 = 5,040,000

16,415 ÷ 5,040,000 = 0.003

ii. Urban Water Use

0.22 × 7,000,000 = 1,540,000

16,415 ÷ 1,540,000 = 0.011

SECTION 3

Big Bad Data Centers

3A

Yes data centers have been around for decades, but I don't think you can compare the effects of the new centers to the original ones. After discussing with others, most agree that the negative environmental effects are the greatest concern. The quality of life for people living nearby data centers has already been distributed. The water quality has worsened and light pollution as well has increased. There might've already been these issues, but the magnitude of the issues has become disastrous. And this is only in a few short years. People feel if data centers are allowed to grow unrestricted and rapidly in the next few years, it will eventually be very difficult to stop. There is an exponential nature to their growth. Another concern is that the benefits of these data centers aren't worth all the environmental costs. What part of data centers actually benefit human society? Sure, some are important for research or healthcare, but most view AI as a way for students to cheat on assignments, make grocery lists, or other activities that require low levels of intelligence. These aren't neutral activities that don't affect others. Through the misuse of AI, people have lost the ability to think for themselves. Continuing down this path will have irrevocable impacts to society. With coal fired power plants, at least we get energy out of them. What we get out of AI can hardly be argued as a benefit.

3B — Meliora Data Center Project

i. Econ 108

The benefits are missing, the environmental problems are the opportunity costs of what. What are the proposed benefits of building a data center, what jobs and economic growth would it bring to the area. Thinking of it as U of R and URMC, what are the benefits of receiving higher education or access to high-level medical care?

ii. Econ 207/238

We become less concerned with the environmental burdens when the benefits are greater than the costs. The institutions in control of these structures pose some responsibility in knowing their environmental impacts, but it's more so the responsibility of the state or federal agency in charge of monitoring water use and greenhouse gas emissions. For example, the dispute over foreign-owned farms in Arizona. The Arizona Department of Water Resources doesn't measure how much groundwater is being pumped in the use of these farms, and are now being scrutinized for being unaware. It's not enough to expect owners to accurately measure and report their environmental burdens. Genuine economic problems surrounding this issue would be the amount of electricity used. These new data centers are using much more electricity compared to their original counterparts, or even compared to UR. From the information provided, the data center would use 6x the amount that UR currently uses which is cause for concern. UR supports the university, hospitals, and widespread offices over the Rochester region. This huge increase in electricity needed would also drive up the price of electricity for the rest of the area. Based on a simple supply and demand curve, if the demand increased so would prices. The land use of the data center would probably just be large-scale resource use. While large, it is comparable to other large scale projects like UR. In addition, Kodak and Xerox complexes could be converted which are already present in Rochester. What is still unclear would be the effects on jobs and wages in the area. You would need more information on the labor supply and demand that the data center would generate.

iii. Econ 300

Communities that support development like this would see economic boom initially. More jobs, higher wages, and environmental effects not yet realized. The increase in capital from a data center would see an increase in labor demand and wages. But as the labor supply increases from the draw of the data center, the marginal product of labor decreases, decreasing wages again. Eventually the data centers may be able to substitute capital for labor. The communities that don't adopt this new technology immediately wouldn't have the need for these new jobs. The labor demand wouldn't exist and therefore more unemployment would occur. If this community eventually allowed data centers years after the first, they would see a greater growth rate due to a catch-up effect.

NEWSPAPER COMMENTARY

“Massive Data Center Will Require as Much Electricity as an Entire City”

A new data center is being built in Rochester. With this new development comes lots of questions about what will be affected locally and regionally. To start, data centers are not a new invention. They have been around for decades, so why all of a sudden is there all this dialogue surrounding them? The most talked about issue is the environmental concerns with these new data centers. The local areas where they are built reap the negative effects such as land use, a decrease in water quality and light pollution. The greenhouse gas emissions and energy use is also concerning. In this case, the data center will be replacing the current Kodak complex. There will be no land use changes in this instance. Concern about new development or removal of forest is not legitimate here. The reuse of these abandoned buildings will be very beneficial to the Rochester area, bringing back jobs to the city. The new data center will have a high demand for jobs with a raise in the wage rate so draw in labor. The years to come will show how much of a permanent change this may be. There is a chance that with this new technology and development, the capital may overcome the labor needed and job demand may drop. I would be cautious assuming any changes will last a while.

The electricity use of the data center is the greatest issue for the Rochester-area. Using the same energy equivalent to an entire city is worrisome. To put it in perspective, this is roughly 6x the amount of energy the University of Rochester and its buildings over the greater Rochester area use. The increase in demand for energy has the potential to raise electricity prices for all as well.

The question here is whether or not you believe in the benefits that data centers may bring. Artificial Intelligence has the capacity to help society. Artificial Intelligence is gaining attraction for its uses in healthcare and research, but what fraction of these data centers is that going towards. What about people’s daily uses for AI? Students use ai for their homework and assignments and others use it to plan groceries and plans for the week. Maybe even worse, people use AI for generating pointless images countless times that become nothing more than a 5 second laugh or like on social media. The argument is, is this benefiting human kind? Do you feel that you are better off from it? There is a loss of intellectual thinking that is being replaced by ai thinking for you. There is a loss of humanity when we don't have to be human to achieve something.
